## CHAPTER 1: CENTRAL WAR-PLANNING AND COUNTER-OFFENSIVE THESIS

### 1.1 Shift from Static Attrition to Proactive Interdiction

The core war planning framework of the Indian Army has completely transitioned from a historic legacy of passive border protection to an active **Defensive-Offensive Framework**. Under old operational directives, India's forces were positioned to absorb an initial enemy attack within heavily fortified border structures, trading space for time, and then mobilizing massive Corps-level formations to strike back over weeks.

The contemporary directive changes this calculation entirely. The Indian Army works under the assumption that modern localized conventional conflicts will be intense, highly destructive, and cut short by international diplomatic interventions. Consequently, India's active war plans rely on launching pre-emptive or immediate proactive cross-border counter-offensives within 12 to 24 hours of detected mobilization or border violation. The goal is to immediately damage the enemy's logistical depth, capture highly defensible tactical terrain across the boundary, and compromise the enemy's air defense networks.

### 3.1 Geographic Command Restructuring

The Indian Army is deployed across six geographic operational commands and one training command, governing 1.2 million active troops:

1. **Northern Command (HQ Udhampur):** Responsible for guarding Jammu, Kashmir, and Ladakh. It manages the Line of Control (LoC) against Pakistan and the Western Sector of the LAC (Aksai Chin/Ladakh) against China.


2. **Western Command (HQ Chandimandir):** Responsible for protecting the plains of international border sectors across Punjab, Haryana, and Jammu.


3. **Eastern Command (HQ Kolkata):** Controls the mountain divisions guarding Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh along the Middle and Eastern sectors of the LAC.


4. **Southern Command (HQ Pune):** Manages the desert tracking networks of southern Rajasthan and Gujarat.


5. **South Western Command (HQ Jaipur):** Operates as an offensive strike command covering the semi-desert sectors of Rajasthan and Punjab.


6. **Central Command (HQ Lucknow):** Acts as a strategic depth command and secures the central sector of the LAC (Uttarakhand/Himachal Pradesh).


7. **Army Training Command (ARTRAC, HQ Shimla):** Responsible for updating military doctrines, standardized training, and tactical war-gaming simulations.



### 3.2 The Integrated Battle Group (IBG) Operational Framework

To eliminate the delays associated with mobilizing large Corps-level formations, India is reorganizing its combat forces into **Integrated Battle Groups (IBGs)**.

* **Structure:** IBGs are modular, self-sufficient brigade-sized forces led by a Major General. They bring together 3 to 4 infantry or mechanized battalions, 2 artillery regiments, an armored component, an engineer element, and a dedicated air defense unit into a single command.


* **Specialization:** They are tailored to their specific theater. *Plains IBGs* focus on heavy armor and mechanized infantry for rapid thrusts. *Mountain IBGs* prioritize light, agile mountain infantry, logistics drones, and lightweight field artillery.


* **Deployment Speed:** Because they are permanently stationed near forward lines with integrated support elements, IBGs can cross starting lines within 12 to 24 hours of an order, skipping the lengthy mobilization timelines of traditional strike corps.

## CHAPTER 4: TERRAIN APPRECIATION ENTITIES (COA, BUA, MOBILITY CORRIDORS)

### 4.1 Critical Obstacle Approach (COA) / Avenue of Approach (AA)

An **Avenue of Approach (AA)** is a ground or air route available to an attacking force that leads toward an objective or key terrain. When an AA contains significant geographical barriers—such as deep river channels, marshlands, or steep mountain valleys—it is classified as a **Critical Obstacle Approach (COA)**.

Planners use a **Modified Combined Obstacle Overlay (MCOO)** to evaluate these routes, calculating terrain trafficability with a standardized formula:

$$\text{MCOO} = \text{Hydrographic Disruption Layer} + \text{Digital Elevation Slope Analysis} + \text{Soil Trafficability Index} + \text{Vegetation Density Vector}$$

```
  [ SLOPE CONTOUR ] ──────► >30% Slope = Stalls Heavy Tracked Armor.
  [ SOIL INDEX ]    ──────► Soft Clay Alluvium = Slows Cross-Country Speed.
  [ HYDRO LAYER ]   ──────► Wide Canal Networks = Restricts Movement to Bridges.

```

* **Slope Limits:** Tracked armor can scale slopes up to 30%, while standard wheeled supply trucks face difficulties on slopes steeper than 15%. Specialized mountain infantry can operate on steep slopes up to 60% by using mountaineering gear and fixed ropes.
* **Soil Trafficability:** Alluvial clay or wet soils reduce the maximum speed of heavy vehicles, forcing armor to stick to predictable, hard-surfaced roads that are easily monitored by enemy defense forces.

### 4.2 Built-Up Areas (BUA)

A **Built-Up Area (BUA)** refers to cities, industrial parks, and towns that modify the natural terrain. In modern operations, BUAs act as strategic defensive positions due to several factors:

* **Line-of-Sight Blockage:** Solid brick and concrete structures disrupt radio signals and block direct-fire weapons, reducing the effective range of long-range anti-tank guided missiles (ATGMs).
* **Defensive Asymmetry:** Entrenched infantry squads inside concrete buildings can hold off much larger attacking forces. This increases the standard $3:1$ attacking force ratio required for open country up to $6:1$ or $10:1$ in urban areas.


* **Three-Dimensional Combat Zones:** Fighting inside a BUA occurs across three distinct levels simultaneously: subterranean networks (utility tunnels, sewers), ground-level streets, and elevated structural positions (high-rise windows, roofs).

* **Mapping Bridgehead Ingress:** Planners mark the exact points where an adversary might deploy armored vehicle-launched bridges (AVLBs). Defensive lines are positioned to cover these potential crossing points with pre-registered artillery fire.
* **Urban Pivots:** Major BUAs like Akhnoor, Samba, and Jammu serve as defensive hubs. If an enemy armored thrust attempts to bypass these built-up areas, garrisoned Indian infantry can launch flank attacks against their supply lines, preventing a deep advance.



#### Desert Sectors (Thar Desert, Jaisalmer, Barmer)

In the Thar Desert, high sand dunes act as natural terrain barriers that restrict heavy wheeled vehicles. Planners focus on mapping the flat, hard salt pans (*Ranns*) located between dune chains, which function as open mobility corridors. Indian forces deploy long-range anti-tank guided missile teams along the edges of these corridors to ambush advancing enemy armor entering the open areas.

### 6.1 Map Exercises (MAPEX) and TEWTs

At the **Army War College** in Mhow, officers participate in regular **Map Exercises (MAPEX)**.

* **Process:** Officers are given realistic tactical problems on large-scale maps and must practice plotting Avenues of Approach, calculating vehicle movement rates through mobility corridors, and planning urban defenses within BUAs.


* **Tactical Exercises Without Troops (TEWT):** Commanders analyze real terrain features on-site without deploying units, practicing how to position assets, establish lines of fire, and coordinate communication networks based on the actual landscape.
